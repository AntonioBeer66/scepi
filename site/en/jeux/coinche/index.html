Coinche, explained

A card game for four, in two teams of two (players sitting opposite each other are partners), played with a 32-card deck (7 to Ace).

The goal

One team announces a contract (“we’ll make at least 100 points”) and then tries to fulfill it. If it succeeds, it scores the points it bid; if it fails, the opposing team scores 160 points. Coinche and surcoinche multiply these gains. The first team to reach 1,010 points wins.

Card strength and value

In trump: Jack (20 points), 9 (14), Ace (11), 10 (10), King (4), Queen (3), 8 and 7 (0), from strongest to weakest.
In the other suits: Ace (11), 10 (10), King (4), Queen (3), Jack (2), 9, 8 and 7 (0).

Bidding

In turn, each player bids a number of points (from 80 to 160, in steps of 10) and a trump suit, or passes. Above that, in this order: the capot (250, the team wins all eight tricks), the capot + belote (270, all eight tricks and the belote), the Générale (250: the taker wins all eight tricks alone, with no trick for their partner) and the Générale + belote (270: the same, with the belote). On a Générale, the taker leads the first trick. Each bid must beat the previous one; you may outbid your own partner. Three passes after a bid close the bidding: the team that made the last bid must reach its contract. Four passes with no bid: the cards are redealt.

Coinche and surcoinche

During the bidding, an opponent of the taker can “coinche”, even out of turn: the points at stake double and the bidding closes. The team that made the bid then has 10 seconds to “surcoinche”: the points quadruple.

Playing the cards

The player after the dealer leads (on a Générale, the taker). You must follow the suit led if you can. When trump is led, you must go higher (play a stronger trump) if possible, even over your partner. If you cannot follow suit, you must trump, and overtrump if a trump is already on the table (otherwise play a lower trump), unless your partner is winning the trick: then you play whatever you like. With neither the suit nor a trump, any card.

The belote

The King and Queen of trump in the hand of the taker or their partner are announced automatically when played (“Belote” on the first, “Rebelote” on the second). If the team that took the bid makes at least 81 points in tricks, the belote adds 20 points to its count for the deal, which helps it reach its contract; it adds nothing to the game score. On defense, the belote does not count.

Scoring

162 points are shared out on each deal (152 in the cards + 10 for the last trick). To make an 80 contract, you need at least 82 points in tricks. The capot requires the team to win all eight tricks, the Générale requires the taker alone to win all eight; their belote versions also require the belote, with no fallback to 250 on failure.

Time

You have 20 seconds to bid or play a card: after that, the computer passes or plays a random legal card for you. A seat left mid-game is taken over by the computer, and you can come back and sit in it again.

How the bots play

All the bots play with the same system of bids and signals, and read yours with the same grid, allowing for a human who does not follow it to the letter. They never see your hand: everything they know about you comes from your bids and the cards played. In the same situation, a bot always makes the same decision, whatever device is running the table.

Opening: the number is a code

  • 80: a trump holding (three trumps with the Jack, three trumps with the 9 and the Ace, four trumps with the Ace and the 10, or the Jack with the belote) and an outside Ace; with no Ace, four trumps including the Jack or the 9. It is not a promise of 80 points. The bot also opens 80 when its calculations show it winning 7 times out of 10, always with an outside Ace. In 4th position, after three passes, its 80 can be lighter: at least two trumps including the Jack or the 9, and an Ace.
  • 90: the Jack and the 9 of trump, with at least three trumps, and four or more “losers” (see 110).
  • 100: length. Four trumps with the Jack or the 9, plus the Ace or the 10, and an outside Ace (or five trumps); or five trumps with the belote and an Ace. A 100 never has the Jack and the 9 together: it is not “stronger” than the 90.
  • 110, 120, 130: the Jack and the 9 of trump, with 3, 2 or 1 losers: the non-trump cards that will not win a trick by themselves, that is, anything that does not head an Ace, 10, King sequence.
  • 160: the Jack and the 9 with no loser at all. Capot: the same hand with five trumps, if its calculations make it succeed at least 7 times out of 10.
  • If its calculations give its level fewer than 6 chances out of 10, it steps down the levels, then tries another suit, before passing.

Supporting: the raise

When its partner has opened, the bot counts what its hand adds and bids the opening plus that total (160 at most), once per deal.

  • +10 per outside Ace, +20 the Jack of trump, +10 the 9 of trump (with at least two trumps, or even bare on an 80), +20 the belote; on an 80, the Jack and the 9 together are worth 10 more. It only raises with a trump honor (Jack, 9 or Ace) or two trumps.
  • Its raise is read literally: “+20” means 20 points, which may be the Jack, two Aces or the belote. It reads yours the same way.
  • Capot: if its Aces outnumber the losers of your opening (110: 3, 120: 2, 130: 1) and its calculations confirm it, it bids the capot.
  • If its own suit is worth more than the raise, it suggests it.
  • It reads your 90, or your 110 and up, as “I have the Jack”; a simple raise does not say so.

Outbidding the opponent

  • It compares, based on its calculations, what it earns by letting the opponent play versus outbidding, and only outbids if the gap is clear. It then bids the minimum, or its scale’s level if it makes it 6 times out of 10.
  • In its partner’s suit, it needs an honor (Jack, 9 or Ace) or two trumps; in another suit, at least three cards including the Jack or the 9.
  • It can also force: raise to the minimum when its hand is worth that minimum minus 10, with the Jack or the Ace of trump, at most twice per side and per deal.

Coinche and surcoinche

A bot coinches on its turn to speak, instead of passing, when its calculations (possible hands consistent with the bids) give the contract little chance of success: under 32% at 80, 22% at 120, 15% at 160. A coinche therefore does not promise a trump holding. Against a capot or a Générale, it only coinches with a sure trump trick (the Jack, or the 9 second).

It only surcoinches an almost certain contract: 95% success at 80 or 100, 90% at 130, 74% for a capot. Late in the game, the score comes first: against a contract that would make the opponent win, it always coinches; if a coinched failure by its side would make the opponent win anyway, it surcoinches.

Call and refusal

  • Call: a 7 or an 8 deliberately discarded (when it could have thrown another suit), during the first three tricks, in a suit whose Ace has not fallen. It means “I have the Ace, lead this suit again”.
  • Refusal: a King or a Queen discarded the same way: “don’t lead this suit”.
  • The 9, 10, Jack and Ace say nothing, nor does a forced discard (only one suit possible), a discard after the 3rd trick or a card played automatically. Without the Ace, a bot throws a 9 rather than a 7 that would look like a call.
  • The latest signal replaces the previous one: a call erases the refusal for the same suit, and vice versa.
  • The bots read your discards the same way, without ever saying what they make of them.

Leading

  • When its side took the bid: it draws trumps in the first two tricks, then leads the suit its partner called. As a taker at 110 or more with the Jack, it always leads trump.
  • On defense: it does not lead trump, cashes its Aces (except an Ace whose 10 is still out, on the first lead), leads the suit called or announced by its partner, and leads a singleton to trump in later.
  • These are its habits: it only departs from them if its calculations see a clear gain, which happens mostly for a taker at 80 or 90 holding only three trumps.
